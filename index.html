Beach-cleaning rover

A small rover that scoops plastic litter off Lake Michigan beaches, modeled on a Bobcat skid-steer loader.

Role
Hardware lead
Team
2 people (teammate wrote the software)
Dates
June – August 2026
Tools
SolidWorks, laser cutting, 3D printing, soldering
Status
v0 prototype working; chassis rebuild next

The problem

Beach litter is collected by hand. I wanted a rover that could drive on loose sand, pick up bottle-sized trash while letting sand fall through, and carry several pieces before emptying.

Design decisions

Sand was the main constraint. It gets into every exposed joint, so fewer moving parts meant a more reliable machine. I studied how Bobcat skid-steer loaders work and compared six options before choosing.

AreaOptionDecision and reason
SteeringSkid steerChosen. No steering linkage, so far fewer joints for sand to clog.
AckermannRejected. More joints exposed to sand.
SuspensionRocker-bogieRejected. Too complex to build with laser cutting and 3D printing.
CollectionLoader scoopChosen. Collects several pieces per pass.
Conveyor beltRejected. Exposed rollers and gears are hard to seal, and it needs far more parts.
Robotic armRejected. Picks up one piece at a time.

How it works

  • A linear actuator on the front plate pushes a crossbar between two pivoting arms, turning its straight-line stroke into arm rotation, the same way a loader's cylinders lift its bucket.
  • A servo tilts the scoop through a pushrod with turnbuckles. The turnbuckles let me adjust the pushrod length to tune the dump angle.
  • The 278 mm scoop has open ribs so sand falls through. I sized it to print in one piece on an Ultimaker S5.
  • The chassis is a 1/2" plywood box (about 360 × 360 × 520 mm) that holds the electronics, with a collection basket on top and a rotating camera mount.

Electrical

I wired and soldered the 12V system: a LiPo battery through a 20 A fuse and a toggle switch, four 37 mm gear motors split left and right across two Cytron MD13S drivers, and a buck converter stepping down to 5 V for the Raspberry Pi. All four motors ran forward and reverse on the bench.

Building it

Before the full chassis, we mounted the drive motors, sand wheels, linear actuator, battery and electronics on a quick v0 base. It worked: running my teammate's vision software on my drive and power system, v0 detected a piece of trash, drove to it and stopped in front of it.

Our first attempt at the laser-cut plywood chassis didn't hold together, so the finger-jointed box is being rebuilt.

Next steps

Rebuild the chassis, add the actuator and pushrod to the CAD, and drive-test on sand before adding the scoop and the vision software.

Baja SAE frame tab fixture

A test fixture for the rear shock tab on Off-Road Illini's car, so the team could check the tab against competition rules.

Dates
January – April 2026
Tools
PTC Creo, FEA
Status
Design and drawings complete

The problem

Baja SAE rule B.12.2.2 requires frame tabs that don't visibly deform under load. The team hadn't tested its tabs since 2021, so each member designed a fixture for one tab. Mine was the rear shock tab.

Design

  • The fixture holds the tab at the same angle and position it has on the chassis, so test results reflect the real car.
  • It had to fit inside the Instron load frame at the campus materials testing lab.
  • I made DXF drawings of the plates for cutting, ready to weld into the fixture.

Analysis

I ran a static simulation at 42 kN, the load from the team's previous test. Maximum displacement was 0.473 mm at the tip of the tab, which makes the tab the weakest point of the assembly, as intended.

What happened next

Welding and physical testing weren't finished before the project wrapped up. Comparing the measured result to the simulation is the next step.

Tools

I measured the existing lower chassis (motors, battery and speed controller) with calipers and designed a deck in Fusion 360 to mount the LiDAR, camera and other sensors above it. Every mounting point fit on the first print.

The first version printed in two pieces, and the joint between them broke. I redesigned it as a single piece, and that version went on the team's car.

Ornithopter wings

Flapping wings for an ASME club ornithopter. The aircraft didn't fly, and finding out why was the most useful part.

Team
ASME Special Projects
Role
Wing design lead, team of 3
Dates
January – April 2026
Tools
Fusion 360, laser cutting
Status
Complete
  • Laser-cut the wing frames from balsa for its stiffness at very low weight, glued with wood glue.
  • Used heavy-duty polyethylene (trash bag) film for the membrane: cheap, strong, and flexible enough to catch air.
  • Set the angle of attack from research suggesting about 60% more thrust than a flat wing. We never measured thrust ourselves.

The ornithopter couldn't lift off because the battery was too heavy. Next time I'd set a weight budget at the start and pick the power source against it.
